Essential, recommended, and optional supplies

These lists follow official kit guidance from Ready.gov (Car Safety and Build A Kit), AAA’s vehicle emergency kit list, the American Red Cross “Emergency Car Kit” article, and NHTSA’s winter and summer “stock your vehicle” lists. The three builds below are ways to cover the essentials and, as you add items, the recommended supplies.

Essential

  • A charged phone, plus a vehicle charger and/or power bank
  • A first-aid kit, and any personal medicines you need (within label temperature limits)
  • A flashlight, plus spare batteries where applicable
  • A tire pressure gauge
  • Warm winter clothing
  • Warning devices: reflective triangles or flares
  • Jumper cables
  • Drinking water and non-perishable food or snacks
  • A blanket (a compact Mylar blanket, a cloth blanket, or both)
  • An ice scraper, in places that see ice or snow
  • A map, even if you also use a phone GPS

Recommended

  • A compact shovel, and a traction aid such as sand, salt, or non-clumping cat litter
  • A tire inflator and a temporary tire sealant
  • Work or winter gloves, and a rain poncho
  • A high-visibility safety vest
  • A NOAA weather radio — it receives alerts but generally cannot transmit a distress signal
  • An appropriately rated fire extinguisher suitable for vehicle storage, secured with an appropriate mounting bracket (in a vehicle fire, evacuate, move away and call 911; prioritize personal safety)
  • Duct tape: “For temporary, non-critical repairs, such as securing loose interior trim.”
  • A multi-tool or basic tool kit, wipes or paper towels, and a little cash
  • Windshield washer fluid
  • A high-visibility distress flag
  • Sturdy walking shoes

Optional

  • A lithium jump starter, stored only as the manufacturer instructs
  • A wool or heavy blanket, in addition to a Mylar blanket
  • A tow or recovery strap rated for your vehicle
  • A road atlas, beyond a basic folded map
  • Hand warmers, a whistle, and a change of clothes
  • A seat belt cutter and compatible window escape tool
  • A pen and notepad
  • Sunscreen
  • A waterproof document pouch
  • Pet food and extra water, if a pet rides with you
  • Books, games, or puzzles for children

East Tennessee and mountain winter

This list works anywhere in the United States. If you drive in the Southern Appalachians — around Kingsport, the Tri-Cities, I-26, I-81, and the steep secondary roads that feed them — winter is less about heavy snow accumulation and more about sudden ice, black ice near freezing, and waiting while crews clear interstates and busy state routes first.

  • Keep the gas tank as full as you reasonably can. A fuller tank also helps keep a fuel line from freezing. If you drive an EV or hybrid, start trips with more charge than you think you need; cold weather reduces range.
  • Before a forecast ice storm, move water into the cabin so bottles do not freeze and split in the trunk, and tell someone your route.
  • Pack the shovel, scraper, gloves, blanket, and traction aid (non-clumping litter or sand) where you can reach them without unloading the whole trunk.
  • Call 911 immediately if you are disabled in an active travel lane. Staying in the vehicle is conditional on safety, especially during fire, flooding, or immediate collision hazards. Exit to a safe location when necessary and possible. Run the engine only intermittently, keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow, and slightly open a window to reduce the risk of carbon monoxide exposure. These precautions do not eliminate risk. Never run the engine in a closed space or with the tailpipe blocked. These points come from TEMA, TDOT, and NHTSA.
  • Check road conditions on TDOT SmartWay or 511 before you go. Increase following distance. Black ice is hard to see when the temperature hovers around freezing.

Extreme summer heat

NHTSA’s summer driving tips are a good short checklist in hot weather too. Heatstroke can happen quickly in a parked vehicle — never leave children or pets unattended in one, even with a window cracked. A child’s body temperature rises faster than an adult’s, and the inside of a vehicle can reach deadly temperatures even on a milder day.

  • Before a hot-weather trip, check coolant and the rest of the cooling system, belts and hoses, tire pressure (when the tires are cold), and air-conditioning performance.
  • Carry extra drinking water. Some medications cannot safely be stored in vehicles due to temperature fluctuations; follow each label’s limits.
  • Follow manufacturer storage and charging temperature limits for lithium devices. Remove them from parked vehicles when temperatures may exceed those limits. Secure them during travel, and follow manufacturer recharge intervals.
  • If the vehicle breaks down in extreme heat, get to shade or a cooled building if you can do so safely, call for help, and do not overexert yourself trying to push or repair the vehicle.

Check your vehicle’s existing equipment before purchasing

  • Spare tire or the inflator kit your vehicle came with
  • Jack, lug wrench, and a place to kneel
  • Owner’s manual and the door-jamb tire-pressure number

If your vehicle has a spare tire, practice changing it in a safe location.

Every six months

  • Charge the power bank or jump starter according to the manufacturer’s instructions
  • Test the flashlight and check the extinguisher gauge
  • Rotate water and food, replace expired first-aid items, and check that medicines are still within their temperature limits
  • Confirm you still carry insurance and registration in the form legally accepted by the applicable state, and update contact numbers
  • Check the spare tire’s pressure

TEMA recommends a kit check twice a year. The printable has a refresh calendar for this.
